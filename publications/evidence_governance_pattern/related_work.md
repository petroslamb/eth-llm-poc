## Related Work

Related work on trustworthy LLM analysis sits in three partially connected streams: capability evaluation, governance frameworks, and claim-level verification. Capability benchmarks and hallucination studies establish the core failure mode we observed: fluent outputs can remain confidently wrong (for example, *Trust Me, I'm Wrong* (Simhi et al., 2025)). In parallel, governance and assurance frameworks such as NIST AI RMF (Tabassi, 2023) and the GenAI profile (Autio et al., 2024) define risk-management principles, while safety-case work (for example, *The BIG Argument for AI Safety Cases* (Habli et al., 2025)) argues for structured evidence in high-stakes systems. These are essential foundations, but they typically operate at organization or system-policy level, not at the per-claim artifact level where analysts resolve day-to-day contradictions.

A newer line of work is closer to our operational problem. Claim-level verification papers such as *From Fluent to Verifiable* (Rasheed et al., 2026) and *MedRAGChecker* (Ji et al., 2026) move evaluation from answer-level impressions to explicit claim-level checks. Conflict-aware retrieval/summarization work (for example, *Rethinking All Evidence* (Chen, J. et al., 2025)) further shows that disagreement between sources is a first-class signal, not noise to be smoothed away. In applied domains, evidence-oriented medical LLM work (for example, *Med-R²* (Lu et al., 2025)) provides a useful analogue for ranking evidence quality rather than treating all supporting text as equal. SOP-compliance evaluation work such as *SOPBench* (Li et al., 2025) adds an adjacent but critical finding: many capable tool-using agents still fail procedural and constraint-following requirements during execution, even when outcome-focused evaluation appears strong. The Trust Calibration Maturity Model (TCMM) (Choung, David & Ross, 2023) offers terminology for multi-dimensional trustworthiness characterization at system level, while the holistic evaluation paradigm of HELM (Liang et al., 2023) establishes that calibration — whether a model's confidence tracks its accuracy — is a measurable property distinct from accuracy itself. Together, these papers support the shift from "is the output plausible?" to "which claims are verifiable, with what confidence, under what counterevidence, and under which procedure guarantees?"

Operationally, frameworks like *Audit Cards* (Staufer et al., 2025) and *Evidence-Bound Autonomous Research (EviBound)* (Chen, R., 2025) point toward process-level accountability, but the field is still converging. Most recent papers are emerging contributions rather than settled canon, and many focus on a subset of the workflow (for example, claim extraction or retrieval conflict handling) rather than end-to-end governance loops. To our knowledge, there is still no widely adopted artifact-level pattern that combines: (1) evidence hierarchy, (2) obligation/claim scoring rubric, (3) explicit contradiction register, (4) claim ledger with confidence and counterevidence, and (5) release gating for external claims.

Our contribution is to operationalize that combined pattern in a real protocol-security setting, with inspectable artifacts and explicit limits. The goal is not to claim solved correctness; it is to make trust decisions auditable, disputable, and updateable as evidence changes.
